# Hiring a YouTube editor

## Decide before you post

1. **Which bottleneck are you hiring for?** Long-form editor, Shorts editor, or thumbnail designer. These are three different skill sets. Anyone who claims to be great at all three is usually mediocre at two. Hire long-form first.
2. **Volume.** 1 video a week versus 3 changes whether you need a freelancer or a part-time retainer.
3. **Budget.** Rough freelance market for a 10 to 15 minute talking-head plus screen-recording tech video, **moderate confidence**. Rates vary a lot by region and portfolio:
   - Entry: about $100 to $250 per video
   - Solid mid-level with YouTube retention instincts: about $250 to $600 per video
   - Top-tier with a track record of channel growth: $800 and up per video, often on retainer
   Paying $150 and fixing everything yourself costs more in your hours than paying $400 once.

## Where to find them

YTJobs, Upwork (filter for YouTube-specific portfolios, not wedding or corporate), Contra, X ("hiring a YouTube editor for a 380k tech creator" posts pull strong inbound), and the editors credited on channels you want to look like. Emailing an editor whose work you already admire is the highest-hit-rate channel.

## Job post

> **Hiring: YouTube long-form editor for an AI / tech channel (ex-Google engineer, 380k+ across socials)**
>
> I make 8 to 15 minute videos about AI tools, coding, and tech careers: talking head, screen recordings, some travel B-roll. I need an editor who cares about **retention**, not effects.
>
> **The job:** 1 to 2 videos a week. You get a locked script with visual notes, raw footage in Drive, and a written brief. Rough cut in 48h, polish in 48h, two revision rounds.
>
> **You're a fit if:** you've edited YouTube videos that have real retention data behind them, you can make a screen recording feel fast, and you're comfortable with tech jargon (or keen to learn it).
>
> **To apply:** send (1) 2 or 3 YouTube videos you edited, with a sentence each on what you decided and why, (2) your rate per video, (3) your timezone and weekly capacity. Applications without examples won't be read.
>
> Shortlisted applicants do a **paid** trial edit.

## Paid trial (the actual filter)

Portfolios lie, because you can't see how much the creator fixed. The trial doesn't.

- Pay a fixed trial fee (about $100 to $200, **moderate confidence**) to 3 to 5 shortlisted editors.
- Give all of them the **same** raw footage (5 to 8 minutes of a real past video) plus the edit brief.
- 72-hour deadline. Then give one round of feedback and watch **how they handle it**.

## Scorecard (1 to 5 each)

| Criterion | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Cold open | Did the first 30 seconds pay off the brief's promise? |
| Pacing | Dead air removed, no stretch over about 10 seconds with no visual change |
| Screen recordings | Zoomed and cropped so a phone viewer can read them |
| Taste | Restraint. Effects serve the point, and there's no meme sound spam |
| Followed the brief | Must-keeps kept, must-cuts cut |
| Feedback round | Fixed exactly what was asked, pushed back only where they had a reason |
| Communication | Asked smart questions, hit the deadline |

Hire the highest total with no 1s. **Feedback round and "followed the brief" outweigh raw talent.** A talented editor who ignores briefs will cost you more hours than they save.

## First 30 days

Weeks 1 to 2: one video at a time, give detailed feedback, then add their recurring mistakes to the edit brief template. Week 4: review their average revision rounds. Still at 3 or more means either the brief or the hire is wrong, so find out which.
