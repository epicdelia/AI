# Review: What AI actually replaced at my old Google job (draft v1)

## 1. Voice — Score: 5/10 (FAILS, needs another pass)

Too much of this reads like a keynote, not a voice memo to a friend. Delia hedges before a claim, punctures her own hype, and lands one metaphor then drops it. This draft explains, lists, and lectures instead.

- Quote: "Pile one: AI does it now and I'd trust it. Pile two: AI helps, but I would never let it drive. And pile three, the weird one: stuff AI made *more* valuable."
  Reason: sounds like a slide, not speech.
  Replacement: "Three piles. Does-it-now. Helps-but-don't-trust-it. And the weird one — stuff that got *more* valuable."

- Quote: "I want to be careful here, though. The *draft* is gone. Knowing what to put in it isn't. Hold that thought."
  Reason: "Hold that thought" is a teacher tic.
  Replacement: "The *draft* is gone. Knowing what goes in it isn't. Keep that in your pocket."

- Quote: "So it reviews. I decide. Four doors, it picks one, I check it's the right door."
  Reason: borrows the Jev "four doors" bit, doesn't fit.
  Replacement: "So it reviews, I decide. It's a really confident intern."

- Quote: "Every engineer knows this feeling."
  Reason: generic appeal-to-all-engineers opener.
  Replacement: "You know this feeling if you've shipped anything."

- Quote: "And the more AI writes everybody's code, the more every company needs someone who can stand in the room and say 'here's what happened, here's what we're doing, here's when.'"
  Reason: "stand in the room and say" = LinkedIn thought-leader voice.
  Replacement: "The more AI writes the code, the more someone has to be the human the customer actually believes."

- Quote: "When writing code gets cheap, everyone builds more. More features, more half-finished internal tools."
  Reason: reads like an econ lecture (Jevons paradox), not her.
  Replacement: "When code gets cheap, everyone just builds more junk, faster."

- Quote: "That's what's left. And notice, neither of these is 'writing code.'"
  Reason: "And notice" is instructor phrasing.
  Replacement: "That's what's left. Neither one is writing code. Weird, right?"

- Quote: "Sundar Pichai said on an earnings call that more than a quarter of new code at Google was being generated by AI. [VERIFY: claim 1] That was a while ago. The code part is going to keep shrinking. The judgment part isn't."
  Reason: the closing line is a too-clean aphorism; also she always flags when a stat is the company's own (see Jev excerpt), this doesn't.
  Replacement: "Sundar said on an earnings call over a quarter of Google's new code is AI-generated now. That's their number, not mine, and it's already old news by the time you're watching this."

- Note on repeated tic: "honestly?" appears twice (tests section, outro). Once is her voice, twice starts to look like a filler crutch. Cut one.

## 2. Retention

Weakest minute: **8:45–9:40, "So should you still learn to code?"** It's the most generic FAQ-answer stretch in the script — one talking-head visual cue at the top and then nothing for ~55 seconds, and it ends on the unverified Pichai stat right before the outro. That's the exact spot a viewer bails before the payoff.

Also flag: **6:30–8:45, "Explaining it to a stressed human"** (lines 114–120) has zero `[VISUAL]` cues for roughly 45 seconds of straight talking. Combined with the static section above it, that's close to two back-to-back stretches with no visual change — the 90-second rule in the brief is at risk here even though each section alone is under it.

First 30 seconds: does pay off the title. The sticky-note rip-off gimmick plus "the coding goes early... what's left surprised me" is a real hook and matches "What AI actually replaced." Keep the cold open as-is.

Chapter "Gone" (1:30–4:00) is the strongest part of the script — fast, three quick wins, visual rip-offs every ~60–90 seconds. Chapter 2 ("Helps, but never drives") is comparatively flat in pacing next to it; consider tightening the on-call section or adding a visual beat mid-way.
